School teams often use "FBA" and "functional analysis" as if they mean the same thing. They do not, and the difference matters when a team is deciding how much assessment a student actually needs.

A functional behavior assessment (FBA) is the broader process of gathering information about when and why a behavior occurs so the team can design a function-based support. A functional analysis (FA) is one specific method that can sit inside that process: the behavior analyst systematically arranges antecedents and consequences and measures whether the behavior changes across those conditions (Iwata et al., 1982/1994; Peterson & Neef, 2020). Every functional analysis is part of an FBA. Most school FBAs do not include a functional analysis.

This post is not a general guide to the FBA process. For that, start with [Functional Behavior Assessment in Public Schools](/blog/functional-behavior-assessment-in-public-schools). For turning a confirmed hypothesis into a plan, see [Function-Based Behavior Intervention Plans](/blog/function-based-behavior-intervention-plans). This post focuses on one narrower question: once the team has a working hypothesis, how do you decide whether to test it experimentally or move to intervention?

## What each type of assessment can and cannot tell you

Peterson and Neef (2020) group FBA methods into three types: indirect assessment, descriptive assessment, and functional (experimental) analysis. They arrange these on a continuum, with indirect methods easiest to use and functional analysis yielding the most precise information (p. 631). The practical question is what each one can support.

### Indirect assessment

Indirect methods include interviews, checklists, rating scales, and questionnaires completed by people who know the student (Peterson & Neef, 2020, pp. 639-640).

**What it can tell you:** what people who see the behavior report about its form, timing, triggers, and typical outcomes. A good interview also helps define the behavior and decide where to observe.

**What it cannot tell you:** whether those reported events actually maintain the behavior. Peterson and Neef (2020) caution that closed-ended rating instruments such as the MAS and QABF have been repeatedly shown to be unreliable and are of questionable validity for identifying function (p. 641). Hanley (2012) recommends open-ended interviews as a way to design a functional analysis, but advises that treatment should typically not be designed from interview results alone.

### Descriptive assessment

Descriptive methods involve direct observation under naturally occurring conditions, such as ABC recording and scatterplots (Peterson & Neef, 2020, p. 635).

**What it can tell you:** what events actually precede and follow the behavior in the student's real routines, how often, and at what times of day. That is useful for refining definitions, identifying high-risk routines, and narrowing hypotheses.

**What it cannot tell you:** whether a consequence that often follows the behavior is the one maintaining it. Peterson and Neef (2020) note that descriptive assessments tend to produce false positives for attention, because adults often attend to severe behavior whether or not attention is the reinforcer, and false negatives for escape, because adults may stop presenting the demands that evoke the behavior (p. 635). Thompson and Iwata (2007) compared descriptive and functional analyses for 12 participants and found that in only 3 of the 12 cases was problem behavior maintained by the consequence observed most often in the descriptive analysis.

That finding does not make ABC data useless. It means a descriptive pattern is a hypothesis, not a demonstration.

### Functional analysis

In a functional analysis, test conditions present a motivating operation and a potential reinforcer contingent on the behavior, and a control condition provides the reinforcer freely with no demands (Peterson & Neef, 2020, p. 632). The team then compares levels of behavior across conditions.

**What it can tell you:** whether a specific variable is functionally related to the behavior. Peterson and Neef (2020) describe functional analysis as the only FBA method that allows practitioners to confirm hypotheses about those functional relations (p. 631). Functional analysis methodology has been replicated across a large research literature (Hanley et al., 2003).

**What it cannot do on its own:** account for every variable in the natural setting. An analysis may miss idiosyncratic variables that are not represented in its conditions, and the process can temporarily increase the behavior (Peterson & Neef, 2020, p. 633). It also requires time, training, and a safe setting.

Peterson and Neef (2020) also make a point school teams sometimes miss: "FAs should be flexible and individualized" (p. 632). A functional analysis is not one fixed protocol with four mandatory conditions.

## Modified formats that fit school constraints

Several variations were developed to make experimental analysis more practical. Each has tradeoffs.

- **Brief functional analysis.** Only one or two short sessions per condition, sometimes followed by a contingency reversal in which the putative reinforcer is moved from the problem behavior to an appropriate replacement behavior (Northup et al., 1991; Peterson & Neef, 2020, p. 634). Peterson and Neef note that a brief analysis reveals a function clearly in fewer cases than a full analysis.
- **Trial-based functional analysis.** Short test and control trials are embedded in the student's normal classroom activities. Bloom et al. (2011) evaluated this approach with 10 students in classroom settings and found correspondence with standard functional analyses in 6 of the 10 cases and partial correspondence in a seventh. Peterson and Neef (2020) note that exposure to the establishing operation in each trial may be too brief to evoke or maintain the behavior for some students (p. 634).
- **Interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis (IISCA).** Information from an open-ended interview is used to build a single test condition in which several suspected contingencies are delivered together, compared with a control condition in which those same reinforcers are available continuously (Hanley et al., 2014; Peterson & Neef, 2020, p. 634). Jessel et al. (2016) described 30 applications that averaged 25 minutes of analysis. Hanley et al. (2014) worked with three children with autism in an outpatient clinic, not a school, and Peterson and Neef (2020) describe the research comparing the IISCA with standard functional analysis as limited and conflicting (p. 634).
- **Latency-based and precursor analyses.** When each occurrence is dangerous, the analysis can end each session at the first response and measure latency, or it can target a precursor behavior that reliably comes before the dangerous behavior (Peterson & Neef, 2020, p. 634).

None of these is a universal default. The right format depends on the question, the behavior, and what the school can staff safely.

## Decision rules for school teams

These rules are a practical framework for team discussion, not a professional standard. Behavior analysts disagree about how often functional analysis should be used. Hanley (2012), for example, reviews commonly cited obstacles to functional analysis, such as time, complexity, and risk, and describes tactics for overcoming them. Use the rules below to make the reasoning explicit and documented, then apply clinical judgment.

### Rule 1: Clear, consistent hypothesis and low risk: test it through the intervention

Move to a function-based plan without a formal functional analysis when all of these are true:

- Interview and observation data point to the same function.
- The pattern is consistent across several observations and routines.
- The behavior is not dangerous to the student or others.
- No prior function-based plan for this behavior has failed.

Treat the plan as the test. Write the hypothesis down, define the measure, collect baseline, and set a review date. If the behavior does not change when the plan is implemented with fidelity, that result tells you the hypothesis may be wrong. Remember that this is a weaker test than a functional analysis, because many things change at once when a plan starts.

### Rule 2: Conflicting hypotheses: test before you plan

When interviews and observations point to different functions, or different staff describe different patterns, a stronger test is usually worth considering. Thompson and Iwata (2007) show how often the most frequent consequence in descriptive data can mislead. A plan built on the wrong function can strengthen the behavior it targets (Peterson & Neef, 2020, p. 630).

### Rule 3: Severe or dangerous behavior: consider an analysis, with safety planning first

It is tempting to rule out a functional analysis whenever behavior is dangerous. Hanley (2012) argues the reverse: the more dangerous the behavior, the more important it is to identify its function accurately, and the central risk question is whether the student would be at greater risk in the analysis than during a typical school day. Precursor analyses, latency-based measures, short sessions, protective arrangements, and clear termination criteria can reduce risk (Hanley, 2012; Peterson & Neef, 2020, pp. 634-635). If the school cannot provide trained staff and a safe setting, the analysis should not proceed there (Peterson & Neef, 2020, p. 633). In that case, document why, and consider whether a precursor analysis or a referral is appropriate.

### Rule 4: A function-based plan already failed: reexamine the hypothesis

If a plan built on the current hypothesis was implemented with fidelity and did not work, the hypothesis is the first thing to question. Check fidelity before blaming the function. If fidelity was adequate, an experimental test is a reasonable next step rather than writing a new plan on the same evidence.

### Rule 5: Low-rate behavior: do not assume an analysis is impossible

Low-rate behavior is hard for every method. Descriptive data may capture only a few episodes. Peterson and Neef (2020) list infrequent behavior as a limitation of functional analysis (p. 633), while Hanley (2012) notes that repeatedly arranging the relevant establishing operations can produce differentiated results even for reportedly low-rate behavior, and that session lengths can be extended. A synthesized or trial-based format informed by a careful interview may be worth considering. So may a longer descriptive period targeted at the routines where episodes cluster.

### Rule 6: The result would not change the plan: skip it

If every plausible function would lead to the same support, a functional analysis adds cost without changing the decision. Assess to the depth the decision requires.

## Before any functional analysis in a school

Even when the decision rules point toward an analysis, these conditions should be in place:

- **Consent and authorization.** Follow district policy for informed consent from the parent or guardian, and confirm the analysis is covered by the assessment plan the team agreed to.
- **Competence.** The person designing and interpreting the analysis should have the training to do it, and implementers should be trained before sessions start.
- **Staffing.** Running sessions in a classroom may require additional staff to supervise other students (Peterson & Neef, 2020, p. 634).
- **Safety plan.** Define termination criteria, blocking procedures, and what happens if the behavior escalates (Peterson & Neef, 2020, pp. 634-635). Involve medical professionals when self-injury is a concern (Hanley, 2012).
- **District policy.** Some districts have specific rules about assessment procedures, restraint, and crisis response. Check them before you write the protocol, not after.

## What IDEA does and does not say

Under the IDEA regulations, a student with a disability who is removed from the current placement under certain discipline provisions must receive, as appropriate, a functional behavioral assessment and behavioral intervention services and modifications designed to address the behavior (34 C.F.R. § 300.530(d)(1)(ii)). When the team determines that conduct was a manifestation of the disability, the IEP team must conduct an FBA, unless one was conducted before the behavior that led to the change of placement, and implement a behavioral intervention plan, or review and modify an existing plan as necessary (34 C.F.R. § 300.530(f)(1)).

The regulation requires an FBA in these situations. It does not specify what methods an FBA must include. Whether to include a functional analysis is a professional decision, shaped by state rules and district policy. For more on routing referrals and when an FBA is needed at all, see the [FBA Decision Matrix](/blog/fba-decision-matrix-for-schools).

## Quick checklist

- [ ] The behavior is defined in observable terms.
- [ ] The team has written the decision this assessment needs to support.
- [ ] Indirect and descriptive data are summarized, and the working hypothesis is written down.
- [ ] Hypotheses agree across informants and observations, or the conflict is documented.
- [ ] Severity and safety risk have been assessed.
- [ ] Any prior function-based plan and its fidelity have been reviewed.
- [ ] The team has asked whether the result would change the plan.
- [ ] If an analysis is planned: format chosen and justified, consent obtained, staff trained, safety plan and termination criteria written, district policy checked.
- [ ] If no analysis is planned: the intervention has a baseline, a measure, and a review date.

## Closing thought

The point is not to run more functional analyses or fewer. It is to make the choice on purpose, write down why, and revisit it when the data say the hypothesis was wrong.
